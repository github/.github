</section><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2 section-heading" onclick="mfTempOpenSection(1)"><span class="indicator mf-icon mf-icon-expand mf-icon--small"></span><h2 id="Definitions">Definitions</h2><span class="mw-editsection">
<a role="button" href="/w/index.php?title=Light-year&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Definitions" class="cdx-button cdx-button--size-large cdx-button--fake-button cdx-button--fake-button--enabled cdx-button--icon-only cdx-button--weight-quiet ">
    <span class="minerva-icon minerva-icon--edit"></span>
<span>edit</span>
</a>

</span>

</div><section class="mf-section-1 collapsible-block" id="mf-section-1">
<p>As defined by the <a href="/wiki/International_Astronomical_Union" title="International Astronomical Union">International Astronomical Union</a> (IAU), the light-year is the product of the <a href="/wiki/Julian_year_(astronomy)" title="Julian year (astronomy)">Julian year</a><sup id="cite_ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>note 1<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> (365.25 days, as opposed to the 365.2425-day <a href="/wiki/Gregorian_calendar" title="Gregorian calendar">Gregorian year</a> or the 365.24219-day <a href="/wiki/Tropical_year" title="Tropical year">Tropical year</a> that both approximate) and the <a href="/wiki/Speed_of_light" title="Speed of light">speed of light</a> (<span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7008299792458000000♠"></span>299<span style="margin-left:.25em;">792</span><span style="margin-left:.25em;">458</span> m/s</span>).<sup id="cite_ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>note 2<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> Both of these values are included in the <a href="/wiki/Astronomical_constant" title="Astronomical constant">IAU (1976) System of Astronomical Constants</a>, used since 1984.<sup id="cite_ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>6<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> From this, the following conversions can be derived:
</p>
<dl><dd><table>

<tbody><tr>
<td rowspan="6" valign="top">1 light-year  
</td>
<td>= <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7015946073047258080♠"></span>9<span style="margin-left:.25em;">460</span><span style="margin-left:.25em;">730</span><span style="margin-left:.25em;">472</span><span style="margin-left:.25em;">580</span><span style="margin-left:.25em;">800</span></span> metres (exactly)
</td></tr>
<tr>
<td><abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7000946100000000000♠"></span>9.461</span> <a href="/wiki/Petametre" class="mw-redirect" title="Petametre">petametres</a>
</td></tr>
<tr>
<td><abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7000946100000000000♠"></span>9.461</span> trillion (<a href="/wiki/Long_and_short_scales" title="Long and short scales">short scale</a>) kilometres (<span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7000587900000000000♠"></span>5.879</span> trillion miles)
</td></tr>
<tr>
<td><abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7004632410770000000♠"></span>63<span style="margin-left:.25em;">241</span>.077</span> <a href="/wiki/Astronomical_unit" title="Astronomical unit">astronomical units</a>
</td></tr>
<tr>
<td><abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="6999306601000000000♠"></span>0.306<span style="margin-left:.25em;">601</span></span> <a href="/wiki/Parsec" title="Parsec">parsec</a>
</td></tr></tbody></table></dd></dl>
<p>The abbreviation used by the IAU for light-year is "ly",<sup id="cite_ref-IAUgen_2-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-IAUgen-2"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>2<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> International standards like <a href="/wiki/ISO_80000" class="mw-redirect" title="ISO 80000">ISO 80000:2006</a> (now superseded) have used "l.y."<sup id="cite_ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>7<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>8<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> and localized abbreviations are frequent, such as "al" in French, Spanish, and Italian (from <i>année-lumière</i>, <i>año luz</i> and <i>anno luce</i>, respectively), "Lj" in German (from <i>Lichtjahr</i>), etc.
</p><p>Before 1984, the <a href="/wiki/Tropical_year" title="Tropical year">tropical year</a> (not the Julian year) and a measured (not defined) speed of light were included in the IAU (1964) System of Astronomical Constants, used from 1968 to 1983.<sup id="cite_ref-Seidelmann_11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Seidelmann-11"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>9<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> The product of <a href="/wiki/Simon_Newcomb" title="Simon Newcomb">Simon Newcomb</a>'s <a href="/wiki/J1900.0" class="mw-redirect" title="J1900.0">J1900.0</a> mean tropical year of <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7007315569259747000♠"></span>31<span style="margin-left:.25em;">556</span><span style="margin-left:.25em;">925</span>.9747</span> <a href="/wiki/Ephemeris_second" class="mw-redirect" title="Ephemeris second">ephemeris seconds</a> and a speed of light of <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7008299792500000000♠"></span>299<span style="margin-left:.25em;">792</span>.5 km/s</span> produced a light-year of <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7015946053000000000♠"></span>9.460<span style="margin-left:.25em;">530</span><span style="margin-left:0.25em;margin-right:0.15em;">×</span>10<sup>15</sup> m</span> (rounded to the seven <a href="/wiki/Significant_digits" class="mw-redirect" title="Significant digits">significant digits</a> in the speed of light) found in several modern sources<sup id="cite_ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>10<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>11<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>12<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> was probably derived from an old source such as <a href="/wiki/Clabon_Allen" title="Clabon Allen">C. W. Allen</a>'s 1973 <i>Astrophysical Quantities</i> reference work,<sup id="cite_ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>13<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> which was updated in 2000, including the IAU (1976) value cited above (truncated to 10 significant digits).<sup id="cite_ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>14<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup>
</p><p>Other high-precision values are not derived from a <a href="/wiki/Coherent_units" class="mw-redirect" title="Coherent units">coherent</a> IAU system. A value of <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7015946053620700000♠"></span>9.460<span style="margin-left:.25em;">536</span><span style="margin-left:.25em;">207</span><span style="margin-left:0.25em;margin-right:0.15em;">×</span>10<sup>15</sup> m</span> found in some modern sources<sup id="cite_ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>15<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>16<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> is the product of a mean Gregorian year (365.2425 days or <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7007315569520000000♠"></span>31<span style="margin-left:.25em;">556</span><span style="margin-left:.25em;">952</span> s</span>) and the defined speed of light (<span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7008299792458000000♠"></span>299<span style="margin-left:.25em;">792</span><span style="margin-left:.25em;">458</span> m/s</span>). Another value, <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7015946052840500000♠"></span>9.460<span style="margin-left:.25em;">528</span><span style="margin-left:.25em;">405</span><span style="margin-left:0.25em;margin-right:0.15em;">×</span>10<sup>15</sup> m</span>,<sup id="cite_ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>17<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> is the product of the J1900.0 mean tropical year and the defined speed of light.
</p><p><span class="anchor" id="abbr"></span>Abbreviations used for light-years and multiples of light-years are:
</p>
<ul><li>"ly" for one light-year<sup id="cite_ref-IAUgen_2-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-IAUgen-2"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>2<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup></li>
<li>"kly" <sup id="cite_ref-Comins_20-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Comins-20"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>18<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> or "klyr"<sup id="cite_ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>19<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> for a kilolight-year (1,000 light-years)</li>
<li>"Mly" for a megalight-year (1,000,000 light-years)<sup id="cite_ref-Hassani_22-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Hassani-22"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>20<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup></li>

</section><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2 section-heading" onclick="mfTempOpenSection(2)"><span class="indicator mf-icon mf-icon-expand mf-icon--small"></span><h2 id="History">History</h2><span class="mw-editsection">
<a role="button" href="/w/index.php?title=Light-year&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: History" class="cdx-button cdx-button--size-large cdx-button--fake-button cdx-button--fake-button--enabled cdx-button--icon-only cdx-button--weight-quiet ">
    <span class="minerva-icon minerva-icon--edit"></span>
<span>edit</span>
</a>

</span>

</div><section class="mf-section-2 collapsible-block" id="mf-section-2">
<p>The light-year unit appeared a few years after the first successful measurement of the distance to a star other than the Sun, by <a href="/wiki/Friedrich_Bessel" class="mw-redirect" title="Friedrich Bessel">Friedrich Bessel</a> in 1838. The star was <a href="/wiki/61_Cygni" title="61 Cygni">61 Cygni</a>, and he used a 160-millimetre (6.2 in) <a href="/wiki/Heliometer" title="Heliometer">heliometre</a> designed by <a href="/wiki/Joseph_von_Fraunhofer" title="Joseph von Fraunhofer">Joseph von Fraunhofer</a>. The largest unit for expressing distances across space at that time was the <a href="/wiki/Astronomical_unit" title="Astronomical unit">astronomical unit</a>, equal to the radius of the Earth's orbit at 150 million kilometres (93 million miles). In those terms, trigonometric calculations based on 61 Cygni's <a href="/wiki/Parallax" title="Parallax">parallax</a> of 0.314 arcseconds, showed the distance to the star to be <span style="white-space: nowrap">660<span style="margin-left: 0.25em">000</span></span> astronomical units (9.9<span style="margin:0 .15em 0 .25em">×</span>10<sup>13</sup> km; 6.1<span style="margin:0 .15em 0 .25em">×</span>10<sup>13</sup> mi). Bessel added that light takes 10.3 years to traverse this distance.<sup id="cite_ref-25" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-25"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>23<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> He recognized that his readers would enjoy the mental picture of the approximate transit time for light, but he refrained from using the light-year as a unit. He may have resisted expressing distances in light-years because it would reduce the accuracy of his parallax data due to multiplying with the uncertain parameter of the speed of light.
</p><p>The speed of light was not yet precisely known in 1838; the estimate of its value changed in 1849 (<a href="/wiki/Hippolyte_Fizeau" title="Hippolyte Fizeau">Fizeau</a>) and 1862 (<a href="/wiki/L%C3%A9on_Foucault" title="Léon Foucault">Foucault</a>). It was not yet considered to be a fundamental constant of nature, and the propagation of light through the <a href="/wiki/Luminiferous_aether" title="Luminiferous aether">aether</a> or space was still enigmatic.
</p><p>The light-year unit appeared in 1851 in a German popular astronomical article by <a href="https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Eduard_Vincenz_Ule" class="extiw" title="de:Otto Eduard Vincenz Ule">Otto Ule</a>.<sup id="cite_ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>24<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> Ule explained the oddity of a distance unit name ending in "year" by comparing it to a walking hour (<i>Wegstunde</i>).
</p><p>A contemporary German popular astronomical book also noticed that light-year is an odd name.<sup id="cite_ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>25<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> In 1868 an English journal labelled the light-year as a unit used by the Germans.<sup id="cite_ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>26<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Arthur_Eddington" title="Arthur Eddington">Eddington</a> called the light-year an inconvenient and irrelevant unit, which had sometimes crept from popular use into technical investigations.<sup id="cite_ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>27<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup>
</p><p>Although modern astronomers often prefer to use the <a href="/wiki/Parsec" title="Parsec">parsec</a>, light-years are also popularly used to gauge the expanses of interstellar and intergalactic space.
</p>
</section><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2 section-heading" onclick="mfTempOpenSection(3)"><span class="indicator mf-icon mf-icon-expand mf-icon--small"></span><h2 id="Usage_of_term">Usage of term</h2><span class="mw-editsection">
<a role="button" href="/w/index.php?title=Light-year&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Usage of term" class="cdx-button cdx-button--size-large cdx-button--fake-button cdx-button--fake-button--enabled cdx-button--icon-only cdx-button--weight-quiet ">

<p>Distances expressed in light-years include those between <a href="/wiki/Star" title="Star">stars</a> in the same general area, such as those belonging to the same <a href="/wiki/Spiral_arm" title="Spiral arm">spiral arm</a> or <a href="/wiki/Globular_cluster" title="Globular cluster">globular cluster</a>. Galaxies themselves span from a few thousand to a few hundred thousand light-years in diameter, and are separated from neighbouring galaxies and <a href="/wiki/Galaxy_groups_and_clusters" title="Galaxy groups and clusters">galaxy clusters</a> by millions of light-years. Distances to objects such as <a href="/wiki/Quasar" title="Quasar">quasars</a> and the <a href="/wiki/Sloan_Great_Wall" title="Sloan Great Wall">Sloan Great Wall</a> run up into the billions of light-years.

</div><section class="mf-section-4 collapsible-block" id="mf-section-4">
<p>Distances between objects within a <a href="/wiki/Star_system" title="Star system">star system</a> tend to be small fractions of a light-year, and are usually expressed in <a href="/wiki/Astronomical_unit" title="Astronomical unit">astronomical units</a>. However, smaller units of length can similarly be formed usefully by multiplying units of time by the speed of light. For example, the <a href="/wiki/Light-second" title="Light-second">light-second</a>, useful in astronomy, telecommunications and relativistic physics, is exactly <span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7008299792458000000♠"></span>299<span style="margin-left:.25em;">792</span><span style="margin-left:.25em;">458</span></span> metres or <style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1214402035">.mw-parser-output .sfrac{white-space:nowrap}.mw-parser-output .sfrac.tion,.mw-parser-output .sfrac .tion{display:inline-block;vertical-align:-0.5em;font-size:85%;text-align:center}.mw-parser-output .sfrac .num{display:block;line-height:1em;margin:0.0em 0.1em;border-bottom:1px solid}.mw-parser-output .sfrac .den{display:block;line-height:1em;margin:0.1em 0.1em}.mw-parser-output .sr-only{border:0;clip:rect(0,0,0,0);clip-path:polygon(0px 0px,0px 0px,0px 0px);height:1px;margin:-1px;overflow:hidden;padding:0;position:absolute;width:1px}</style><span class="sfrac">⁠<span class="tion"><span class="num">1</span><span class="sr-only">/</span><span class="den"><span class="nowrap"><span data-sort-value="7007315576000000000♠"></span>31<span style="margin-left:.25em;">557</span><span style="margin-left:.25em;">600</span></span></span></span>⁠</span> of a light-year. Units such as the light-minute, light-hour and light-day are sometimes used in <a href="/wiki/Popular_science" title="Popular science">popular science</a> publications. The light-month, roughly one-twelfth of a light-year, is also used occasionally for approximate measures.<sup id="cite_ref-39" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-39"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>37<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-40" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-40"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>38<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup> The <a href="/wiki/Hayden_Planetarium" class="mw-redirect" title="Hayden Planetarium">Hayden Planetarium</a> specifies the light month more precisely as 30 days of light travel time.<sup id="cite_ref-41" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-41"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>39<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup>
</p><p>Light travels approximately one foot in a <a href="/wiki/Nanosecond" title="Nanosecond">nanosecond</a>; the term "light-foot" is sometimes used as an informal measure of time.<sup id="cite_ref-42" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-42"><span class="cite-bracket">[</span>40<span class="cite-bracket">]</span></a></sup>
</p>
